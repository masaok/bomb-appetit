Active listening games work when mishearing costs something the players care about. In a bomb defusal round, a misheard instruction becomes a strike and a faster clock, so players start repeating each instruction back before acting. That one habit, practised as a short drill, is what turns listening from a polite idea into a skill.

## Why active listening games need a cost for mishearing

Listening exercises only change behaviour when getting it wrong hurts a little. Without a cost, people nod, assume they understood and move on, because checking feels slower than guessing. Add a visible penalty for a misheard word and checking suddenly becomes the fast option. The cost is what turns a lesson into a habit.

Plenty of party games involve listening. The whisper chain, where a sentence gets passed ear to ear and comes out garbled, is fun precisely because nothing is at stake. Everyone laughs at the result. Nobody leaves the room listening differently, because the game never asked them to do anything with what they heard.

A useful listening game asks for action. Someone hears an instruction, does something with it, and finds out right away whether they heard it correctly. That loop of hear, act and learn is short enough to change what people do next time.

Bomb Appétit has that loop built in. The Defuser is the only player who can see and touch the bomb and cannot look at the manual. The Experts hold the manual and cannot see the bomb. Every move the Defuser makes comes from something an Expert said out loud. When a move is wrong, the bomb records a strike, and the countdown runs faster from then on. After two strikes it runs at one and a half times its normal speed, and the default third strike ends the round.

That penalty is small enough to keep the game fun and large enough that a team notices. It also lands on the whole team at once. Nobody is singled out, which matters if you are using the round with colleagues or a class. The question after a strike is never "who messed up?" but "where did the words go wrong?"

## The repeat-it-back drill with a bomb

The drill is simple: the Expert gives one instruction, the Defuser says it back in full, the Expert confirms or corrects, and only then does the Defuser act. It adds a couple of seconds per move and removes most strikes caused by mishearing. Practise it on an easy bomb until it happens without anyone reminding the team.

The manual itself asks for this. Its opening checklist tells Experts to have instructions repeated back before the Defuser acts, because a wrong move is a strike. The game's how-to-play page puts it as a two-part tip: Experts give one clear instruction, the Defuser says it back before acting.

Here is a way to run it as a deliberate drill rather than leaving it to chance.

1. **Pick an easy bomb.** The First Bite mission has a single module and five minutes on the clock, which leaves room to be slow on purpose.
2. **Agree on the format before starting.** The Defuser repeats every instruction in full, including any number, color and position. The Expert answers with "yes" or a correction, never with a new instruction.
3. **Play one round strictly.** No action without a repeat. If anyone skips it, pause and redo the step, even if the move would have been right.
4. **Swap roles.** The repeat sounds different from the other side. Experts learn what a clear instruction feels like by hearing a vague one said back to them.
5. **Add pressure.** Move to a mission with more modules and less time, such as Quick Snack, which packs four modules into four minutes, and keep the repeat going.

The full repeat matters more than it seems. "Yes, got it" confirms nothing, because the Expert cannot hear what the Defuser understood. "Cutting the fourth wire from the top, the red one" exposes the Defuser's version so the Expert can compare it with their own.

Good repeats also include one extra fact the Defuser can see and the Expert cannot. Adding "the red one" to a position gives the Expert a second chance to catch a mismatch. If the Expert expected a white wire in that slot, something went wrong in the description, and now is the cheapest moment to find out. The post on [describing a module in a fixed order](/blog/communication-games-describing-a-bomb#the-description-habit-shape-then-color-then-position) covers how the Defuser builds that description in the first place.

## How does a misheard instruction become a strike?

A misheard instruction becomes a strike when the Defuser acts on their version of the words instead of the Expert's. The gap usually sits in a small word: "last" against "last red", a count from the wrong end, or two Experts speaking over each other. Repeating back exposes that gap while the wire is still whole.

### A worked example, played twice

This exchange is invented for illustration. To keep the example concrete, assume the Expert's Wires page, on this rule seed, ends on a line telling them to cut the last red wire. On a real bomb the line depends on the seed and on the wires, so treat the rule here as a placeholder.

**Take one, no repeat.**

> **Defuser:** Wires. Five of them. From the top: blue, red, white, red, black.
>
> **Expert A:** Five wires. Okay, reading the five-wire list.
>
> **Expert B:** Do you want me to check the serial?
>
> **Expert A:** No, we don't need it. Cut the last red one.
>
> **Defuser:** Last one. Cutting.

The Defuser heard "cut the last" and "red" as two separate thoughts while Expert B was still talking. They cut the fifth wire, the black one. That is a strike, and the timer now runs faster.

**Take two, with the repeat.**

> **Defuser:** Wires. Five of them. From the top: blue, red, white, red, black.
>
> **Expert A:** Five wires. Reading the five-wire list. Quiet for a second, please.
>
> **Expert A:** Cut the last red wire.
>
> **Defuser:** Cutting the last wire, the black one?
>
> **Expert A:** No. The last _red_ wire. That's the fourth from the top.
>
> **Defuser:** Fourth from the top, red. Cutting.
>
> **Expert A:** Yes.

The same mishearing happened in both takes. In the second, the Defuser said their version aloud and the Expert caught it before anything was cut. Under this example's rule the cut solves the module and its light turns green.

Three small things changed between the takes. Expert A asked for quiet while reading, so Expert B's question did not land on top of the instruction. The Defuser added the color of the wire they were about to cut, which gave the Expert a second fact to check. And the Expert, when correcting, gave the position from the top as well as the color, so the Defuser had two independent ways to find the right wire.

### Other ways the words slip

Counting direction is the classic slip. The Wires manual counts from the top, starting at one, but a Defuser who just said "the bottom one is black" may keep counting from the bottom. Sound-alike words are another: the Word Grid page warns that many of its words sound alike and tells Experts to have the Defuser spell every one. Strike-dependent rules are a third. The Color Echo page picks its column by the current number of strikes, so an Expert who reads from the column they used a minute ago is giving an answer for a bomb that no longer exists.

## Listening as an Expert: hearing the whole description first

Listening as an Expert means hearing the Defuser's whole description before reading any rule aloud. An Expert who starts answering after the first detail has stopped listening to the rest, and the missing detail is often the one the rule needs. Wait for the end, say back what you heard, then look it up.

The manual's rules are written to be read in a particular way. The Wires page has a separate list for each wire count, and the Expert reads from the top of the right list and stops at the first true line. The lines can depend on counts of a color, on the last wire's color, or on the serial number. Every one of those facts comes from the description. If the Expert jumps in after "five wires, from the top: blue, red", they have the right list and half the colors.

Experts can repeat back too, and it is just as useful. "Five wires: blue, red, white, red, black, counted from the top" takes three seconds. It catches a Defuser who misspoke and gives the Expert a clean copy to read rules against. Some teams have the Expert write descriptions down. The Recall module's page goes further and asks Experts to note the position and label pressed at every stage, because later stages ask about earlier ones.

Edgework rewards the same patience. The serial number, batteries, indicators and ports on the case come up in many rules. The manual's appendix says to ask for all of it once, at the start, and write it down. An Expert who listens to the full edgework read-out and repeats the serial number character by character avoids asking again under pressure. The appendix even helps with the repeat: the letters I and O never appear in a serial, so a round character is always a zero.

Several Experts add a different listening problem: listening to each other. If two Experts own different modules, each one needs to hear only the descriptions meant for them and ignore the rest. Saying the module name at the start of each description ("Keypad:" or "Wires:") lets the right person tune in. The post on [how to be a better communicator with lessons from a bomb](/blog/how-to-be-a-better-communicator-lessons-from-a-bomb) covers how to split who speaks.

## Do active listening rounds differ for adults and students?

The drill stays the same for adults and students. What changes is who enforces it and how much pressure to add. Adults can usually agree on the rule and police it themselves. Students usually need a referee to stop a round when a step is skipped, and more time on easy bombs first.

For adults, whether friends at a game night or a team on a call, the main obstacle is pride. People feel silly repeating "cutting the fourth wire from the top" out loud. A good way past that is to make the first round a strict drill, as in the step list above, and to play the second round freely. Comparing strikes and questions between the two rounds makes the case without anyone having to argue it.

For students, structure helps more than persuasion. Groups of three to five fit the game, since it takes one Defuser and one to four Experts. Assign a referee role within each group whose only job is to say "repeat it" whenever an action starts without a read-back. Rotate the Defuser each round so everyone hears instructions from both sides. The post on [a communication skills lesson plan around one bomb](/blog/communication-skills-games-for-students-bomb-lesson#a-communication-skills-lesson-plan-around-one-bomb) lays out timings and group rotations for a full class.

Either group benefits from the same progression. Start on a single-module mission, then two modules, then a mission with a needy module once the repeat is automatic. Needy modules such as the Pressure Vent, which asks a yes or no question with a 40 second countdown, test whether the habit survives an interruption.

## The listening mistake: nodding along and cutting the wrong wire

The typical listening mistake is agreeing before understanding. The Defuser hears most of an instruction, fills in the rest with what seems likely, says "yep" and acts. It feels efficient. It is also how most avoidable strikes happen, because the part that got filled in was the part that mattered.

Nodding along is hard to see from the outside. On a video call, "yep" sounds exactly like comprehension. In a room, a nod looks the same whether the listener caught every word or half of them. That is why the repeat has to be words, not a nod and not "got it". Only words let the speaker check the listener's version.

It also tends to get worse after a strike. The clock is faster, everyone feels behind, and repeating feels like a luxury. In practice the opposite is true. A second strike makes the countdown faster still, and on the default settings a third ends the round, so the moment after a strike is the moment a check is worth the most. The how-to-play page's last tip fits here: a strike is not the end, take a breath, then go faster.

Watch for one more version of the mistake: the Expert who nods along to the Defuser's description. If the Expert says "okay" and starts reading before the description is complete, they are making the same guess from the other side. Both seats need the same discipline.

To build the habit, open [the play page](/play), pick First Bite, and run the drill exactly as written once. Then play a longer mission and count how many times someone said an instruction back. If that number drops as the clock speeds up, you have found the next thing to practise.
