One of the simplest family game night ideas is a cooperative bomb round: one person holds a laptop or tablet with the bomb, everyone else reads a printed manual, and the whole table talks it through. It suits mixed ages because each reader can own a page that matches what they can do, and nobody wins alone.

## Why family game night ideas with one screen beat a pile of boxes

A single screen and a stack of paper beats a cupboard of boxed games because setup takes two minutes, the rules are taught by playing, and the same evening can stretch from a young child's first go to a teenager's hardest attempt. Nobody reads a rulebook aloud before the fun starts.

Bomb Appétit is the game we have in mind here. It runs in a web browser, costs nothing, needs no install and no account. One player, the Defuser, sees a ticking bomb covered in small puzzles called modules. Everyone else, the Experts, holds the manual and never sees the bomb. The bomb needs one device. The manual can sit on a second device or come out of a printer, because the [standard manual at /manual/1](/manual/1) is laid out to print cleanly in black and white with your browser's print command.

That second option is what makes it a one-screen evening. Print the manual once, staple it or leave the pages loose, and hand them round the table. The household's single laptop or tablet goes to whoever takes the bomb. Phones can stay in a drawer.

A boxed game also tends to fix the number of seats. This one takes two to five people: one Defuser and one to four Experts. A parent and one child can play. So can a grandparent, two parents and two children. The game does not change shape when someone joins late; they pick up a page and become an Expert.

There is a smaller practical win too. A family that plays a lot of boxed games knows the clean-up. Here, closing the browser tab is the clean-up, and the printed pages go in a folder for next week.

## Who takes the bomb on family game night?

On family game night, the bomb should go first to the person who describes things clearly and stays calm when a timer runs, and second to whoever is most excited to hold it. Often that is an older child or a teenager. Rotate the seat every round so each person defuses at least once.

The Defuser's job is mostly talking. They describe each module out loud, read the details printed on the sides of the case, and press, cut or turn only when an Expert tells them to. The manual's own glossary calls those case details edgework: the serial number, batteries, indicators and ports. A serial number is six characters and always ends in a digit, so a Defuser needs to read letters and numbers aloud without guessing.

That makes reading age a sensible floor for the Defuser seat. A child who can read "4 wires: red, blue, blue, white" and spell out a short code can defuse. A child who cannot yet read letters will enjoy the Expert side more, especially on the pages built around matching pictures.

Some families like to start with the adult who knows the game as Defuser for one demonstration round. Keep that round short. The [how to play page](/how-to-play) covers the whole briefing in about five minutes, and the first mission has only one module, so a demonstration takes a few minutes before the seat moves on.

If two people both want the bomb, let the one who waited longest go first and promise the other the next round. With a short mission, that wait is a few minutes at most.

## Which modules a younger Expert can read

A younger Expert can read the modules whose manual pages ask for matching a symbol, finding a short line in a table, or counting a few colored wires. Pages that need written notes, spelling sound-alike words or tracking several facts at once suit an older reader. Bomb Appétit has no age rating, so judge by the page.

What follows is reasoning from what each manual page actually asks the Expert to do. Read the pages yourself at [/manual/1](/manual/1) before you assign them, because you know your own child's reading better than any table.

**Glyph Keypad** is the friendliest page for a young Expert. The Defuser describes four symbols, each an outer shape with a mark inside, such as a circle with a dot. The page shows several columns of symbols with names. The Expert finds the one column that holds all four, then calls out the order from top to bottom. That is picture matching and saying an order aloud.

**Pressure Vent** is a needy module, meaning one that cannot be solved and keeps waking up. The Defuser reads a short question aloud, the Expert finds the same question in a two-column table and says Yes or No. The catch is a 40 second countdown, so it suits a child who reads short phrases quickly.

**Wires** asks the Expert to find the list for the number of wires, then read short "if" lines from the top until one is true. The lines count colors ("exactly one red wire"), check the last wire's color, and sometimes ask whether the serial number's last digit is odd or even. Counting is easy for most children. The odd-or-even line is where a reader sitting beside them helps.

**Discharge Lever** needs no Expert at all. Its page says the Defuser holds the lever down to drain a filling meter and lets go when it is empty.

The harder pages for a young reader are Color Echo, Word Grid, Recall, Blinker, Tangled Wires and Wire Panels. Color Echo picks its table by the serial number and its column by the current strike count, and the column changes after a strike. Word Grid asks the Defuser to spell words that sound alike, then a two-step lookup. Recall needs notes written at every stage and thrown away when it resets. Blinker turns short and long flashes into letters, a word and a frequency.

## How do you keep a family bomb round from ending in tears?

Keep a family round calm by choosing short missions with few modules, telling everyone before the start that a loss is normal, and making the adult's job asking questions rather than giving answers. Tears usually come from a child feeling blamed for a strike, so blame the bomb, never the reader.

A strike is a mistake: a wrong cut, a wrong press, a wrong answer. Each strike speeds up the countdown. The first makes it run at 1.25 times its normal speed, the second at 1.5 times, and on most missions the third sets the bomb off. The bomb also goes off when the timer reaches zero. Those rules are on the [how to play page](/how-to-play), and they are worth reading aloud before the first round, so the speeding clock is a known joke rather than a surprise.

Say out loud, before the start, that an early bomb may well explode and that this is normal. That one sentence takes the weight off a child who presses the wrong key. Then, after a strike, the adult says "what did you see?" instead of "why did you do that?".

Losing together is easier than losing alone, and that is the whole point of a shared bomb. The post on [why cooperative games for families make losing bearable](/blog/cooperative-games-for-families-everyone-wins#why-cooperative-games-for-families-make-losing-bearable) explains why a shared result changes the mood at the table.

Short rounds help too. If the evening runs long, a round that ends in an explosion is over in a few minutes and the next one starts straight away. For a plan with more rounds and breaks between them, see [a running order for a bomb defusal game night](/blog/game-night-ideas-for-adults-bomb-defusal#a-running-order-for-a-bomb-defusal-game-night), which works for a family evening as well as an adult one.

Finally, watch the volume. The timer and the strike sound are part of the fun, and some children love them. If one child finds them too much, give that child the manual pages, sit them away from the bomb's screen, and let them be the calm voice.

## Settings and missions for a calm family round

For a calm family round, play the first missions in order, keep the standard manual (rule seed 1), and pick whichever bomb view the device handles better. The opening missions give five minutes for one to three modules and allow three strikes, which leaves room to talk slowly.

Missions are set bombs with a fixed time limit, a set number of modules and a strike limit. The [missions page](/missions) lists all 20 in four sections. The first section is where a family should live for a while:

- **First bite**: one module, Wires, five minutes. The blurb says it plainly: learn to describe what you see.
- **Button up**: Wires plus the Big Button, five minutes.
- **Strange symbols**: Wires, Big Button and Glyph Keypad, five minutes. A good one for a young Expert who owns the symbol page.
- **Table for three** and **Quick snack**: four minutes each, with three and four modules.

The first needy module arrives in the third section, on Pressure cooker, with six minutes for four modules plus a Pressure Vent. Hold that one back until the family has defused the first section more than once.

The rule seed is the number that generates every rule in the manual. Rule seed 1 is the standard manual. The bomb and the manual must use the same seed, so check the number printed on your pages before you start. A different seed gives different rules, and the wrong pages will set off the bomb.

The bomb comes in a 3D view and a 2D view. On an older tablet or a small screen, the 2D view is often easier to handle. The game runs on recent Chrome, Firefox, Safari or Edge, on a computer, tablet or phone, though the bomb is easiest on a bigger screen.

There is also freeplay, where you build your own bomb instead of playing a set mission. For a family that has outgrown the first section but finds the later missions too long, freeplay is the way to make something in between.

Signing in is optional. Guests can play every mission. Signing in with GitHub only adds leaderboards and saved progress, which matters more to a competitive teenager than to a first family evening.

## The family game night mistake: a parent as the only Expert

The most common family game night mistake is seating a parent as the only Expert with the whole manual while the children watch. The parent reads, decides and instructs, the children become an audience, and the round turns into one adult talking to one Defuser. Spread the pages so every person owns something.

It happens for good reasons. The parent has read the rules, wants the round to go well, and does not want a child to feel the pressure of a wrong answer. But a child with nothing to hold drifts off within a minute, and a child who watches a parent solve every module learns that the game belongs to grown-ups.

The fix takes thirty seconds before the round. Split the printed manual into piles, one per Expert. The how to play page suggests exactly this: with several Experts, give each one a few modules to own. A young child takes Glyph Keypad. An older child takes Wires and Big Button. The parent takes the edgework appendix and the harder pages, and writes down the serial number, batteries and indicators as the Defuser reads them out.

Then the parent's role changes. Instead of reading the answer, the parent asks the child: "Which column has all four?" If the child is right, the child says it to the Defuser. If the child is stuck, the parent points at the page rather than taking it.

The same thinking applies when part of the family is in another city. A grandparent can own a page over a call just as a child owns one across the table. The guide to [games to play with family online when you live apart](/blog/games-to-play-with-family-online-living-apart) covers that setup, with a room code and a phone call.

Try one round with the piles split and one with a single adult reader, and compare how loud the table gets. The split round is usually louder, slower at first, and much more fun for the youngest player.
