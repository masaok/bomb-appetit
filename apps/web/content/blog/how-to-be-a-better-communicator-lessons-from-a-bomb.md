To be a better communicator, report what you observe before asking for help, give one instruction and wait for it to land, have the listener say it back, and settle on shared names early. A bomb defusal round teaches all four quickly, because the person acting cannot read the rules and every misunderstanding costs time.

## How to be a better communicator when the clock is running

Under time pressure, better communication means fewer words with more information in each, a clear order of who talks when, and a check before anything irreversible happens. A countdown removes the slack that usually hides sloppy speech, so the habits that work there tend to work anywhere a team is busy.

Most advice about communication assumes calm. You have time to rephrase, to ask a follow-up, to send a message later saying what you meant. A timer takes that away. It also makes the cost of a muddled sentence visible to everyone in the room at once, instead of surfacing a week later as a missed deadline.

Bomb Appétit makes a good practice ground for that reason. One player, the Defuser, can see and touch the bomb but never reads the manual. The other players, the Experts, hold the manual and never see the bomb. Each mistake at the bomb is a strike, and strikes make the countdown speed up: by a quarter after the first, by half after the second. The default third strike sets the bomb off. A team learns quickly which kinds of sentences buy time and which ones burn it.

What follows is five habits, each tied to a moment at the bomb where it either saves the round or loses it. None of them needs talent. They need a little agreement before you start and some discipline once the timer is moving. The game's own [how to play page](/how-to-play) lists short versions of several of them under its communication tips, which is a fair sign they come up in every round.

## Why say what you see before you ask what to do?

Saying what you see first gives the other person the facts they need to answer. Asking "what do I do?" before describing anything hands them a question they cannot answer, so they have to interview you, and the interview takes longer than the description would have. Observation first, request second.

At the bomb this is the Defuser's main job. The how-to-play page puts it in four words: describe first, then ask. Its example is a Defuser saying "Four wires. Red, blue, blue, white." That sentence lets an Expert open the Wires page, find the four-wire list and start reading without asking anything. Compare it with "There are wires, what do I cut?" which forces at least three questions before any rule can be checked.

The same pattern helps outside a game. A support ticket that says "the export fails" starts an interview. One that says "the export fails on files over a certain size, with this error, since this morning" starts a fix. The facts were known to the sender in both cases. Only one version passed them on.

There is a second reason to describe first: it stops the speaker from filtering. A Defuser who guesses which detail matters often leaves out the one the rule checks. Since the rules sit on the Expert's side, and change completely with each rule seed, the Defuser cannot know what is relevant. The safe move is to report everything in a set order and let the person with the rules decide. The post on [why describing a bomb module is hard](/blog/communication-games-describing-a-bomb) goes into that order in detail, with examples of vague and precise descriptions side by side.

Edgework is the clearest case. The labels on the bomb's casing (serial number, batteries, indicators and ports) come up in rule after rule. The manual's opening section asks Experts to collect all of it at the start. A Defuser who reads it out unprompted, before the first module, has answered a dozen future questions in one go.

## One instruction at a time, then wait

Giving one instruction at a time means each sentence carries a single action, and the speaker stops until that action is confirmed. Chained instructions save a second when they work and cost a strike when one link is misheard. Splitting them lets the listener check each step and lets the speaker catch a wrong move before the next one.

Bomb Appétit's own manual is written this way. The project's guide for module authors says manual text should be written for someone reading aloud under pressure, with short sentences and one instruction per sentence. The rules themselves reward the same style.

The Labyrinth module is the clearest example. The Defuser sees a grid of dots with a light and a goal but cannot see any walls. The Expert has the maze. The manual says to guide the light one step at a time, and stepping into a wall is a strike. An Expert who says "up, up, right, down, right" in one breath is gambling that the Defuser counted every word. An Expert who says "up" and waits to hear "moved up" can correct a slip before it compounds.

The Big Button shows why waiting matters even inside one action. Its manual tells the Expert whether to tap or hold. A tap is a press and release in under half a second. When the answer is hold, a colored strip lights up beside the button, and the release depends on that strip's color. So "hold it" has to be followed by a pause while the Defuser reports the strip, and only then by the release instruction. Saying it all up front is impossible, because the second half depends on what the Defuser sees after the first.

Waiting does not mean silence. It means the listener speaks next. The usual confirmation is the listener repeating the instruction before acting, which the manual also asks for in its opening checklist. The post on [the repeat-it-back drill and how to practise it](/blog/active-listening-games-repeat-it-back#the-repeat-it-back-drill-with-a-bomb) turns that into a short exercise you can run before a real round.

## One voice at a time when several Experts know the answer

When several people know the answer, one of them should say it. Two voices giving the same instruction in different words sound like two different instructions, and the listener has to stop and work out whether they agree. Decide in advance who speaks for each part of the task, and have the others stay quiet unless asked.

Bomb Appétit takes two to five players, so a Defuser can have up to four Experts. The manual's intro gives the practical fix: split the pages between Experts so that two people never search for the same thing. The how-to-play tips say the same: give each Expert a few modules to own. Ownership answers the question of who speaks. If the Glyph Keypad belongs to one Expert, that Expert gives the keypad instructions, and everyone else listens.

Overlap is most likely at the moment of agreement. Two Experts reach the same answer on the same page, and both say it, slightly out of sync, with slightly different wording. "Cut the third one" and "third wire, cut it" arrive half a second apart. The Defuser cannot tell if that was one instruction or two. A rule as simple as "the owner speaks, others say 'agree' or nothing" removes the problem.

Needy modules test this habit hardest. They cannot be solved, wake up now and then and need attention fast. The Pressure Vent, for example, asks a yes or no question with a 40 second countdown, and a wrong answer or no answer is a strike. If every Expert drops their page to shout an answer, three modules stall. Give needy modules an owner too, so one person handles the interruption and the rest keep their place.

The same structure works in any meeting with several people who know the material. Name who answers which kind of question. Let others add only what is missing. Teams that run a bomb round at work can use [debrief questions after a team building bomb round](/blog/team-building-communication-games-bomb-debrief#debrief-questions-after-a-team-building-bomb-round) to see whether this happened, and the full write-up on [running a bomb debrief for team communication](/blog/team-building-communication-games-bomb-debrief) covers what to change next time.

## How do you agree on names for symbols and colors?

Agree on names by picking them out loud the first time a symbol or color appears, choosing names both sides can check, and then using only those names for the rest of the round. A name the listener can look up beats a vivid one. Once a name is set, changing it mid-round is as costly as never naming it.

The how-to-play page is blunt about this: pick names for odd symbols and stick with them, because "the squiggly one" only works once. The mission Strange Symbols, which introduces the Glyph Keypad, says much the same in its blurb.

The Glyph Keypad gives a good model for naming. Each key shows a glyph that the manual describes as an outer shape with a mark inside, such as a circle with a dot. The manual's table labels each glyph by those two parts. That means the best name for a glyph is already on the Expert's page. If the Defuser says "hexagon, zigzag inside", the Expert can match it directly. A private name like "the beehive" has to be translated every time it is used.

Colors need the same care. The game uses a small set of named colors, and each color on a module face comes with a letter, so a face never relies on color alone. Pick the plain name and stay with it. If the team calls a wire "black" at the start, nobody should switch to "dark" later.

Some things are better spelled than named. The Word Grid page tells Experts to ask the Defuser to spell every word, because many of its words sound alike. That is a naming agreement too: for this module, the name of a word is its spelling.

At work, the equivalent is a shared glossary that someone actually uses. Agree on what you call each system, report or customer tier, write it down once, and correct drift early. Two names for one thing look harmless until two people act on them differently.

## The communicator's mistake: filling silence with guesses

The most expensive mistake is filling a quiet moment with a guess. When an answer takes a few seconds, someone offers a possibility to keep things moving, and the listener hears it as an instruction. A short, plain "still looking" is better than any guess, because it keeps the line open without sending anyone into action.

At the bomb, silence happens while an Expert reads a page. The Wires list, for example, has to be read from the top until the first line that is true. That takes a few seconds. A Defuser who fills the gap with "is it the red one?" invites a distracted "maybe, yes" and a cut that nobody really ordered. An Expert who fills it with "probably the second wire" hands the Defuser a guess that sounds like an answer.

Guessing also hides the real state of the work. If the Expert says "I think it's the third" when they actually have not found the right list, the team believes it is closer than it is. Saying "I'm on the six-wire list, two lines left" is honest and costs about the same time.

The how-to-play page closes its tips with a reminder that a strike is not the end: take a breath, then go faster. After a strike, the urge to fill silence grows, because the clock is now faster. That is exactly when a calm "checking" helps most.

### Eight phrases for the next round

Each phrase below is an example to adapt, paired with the problem it prevents.

1. **"Here's what I see: ..."** Prevents an interview, because the facts arrive before the question.
2. **"Counting from the top"** Prevents a count from the wrong end.
3. **"Ready, go ahead."** Prevents a description landing while the Expert is still turning pages.
4. **"Say it back to me."** Prevents a misheard instruction turning into a strike.
5. **"That one's mine."** Prevents two Experts answering at once in different words.
6. **"Let's call it the circle with a dot."** Prevents a private nickname that only one person understands.
7. **"Spell it, please."** Prevents confusion between words that sound alike.
8. **"Still looking, give me five seconds."** Prevents a guess filling the silence and being heard as an order.

Try them on a short mission from [the missions list](/missions), then play the same mission again without them. The difference shows up in the number of questions asked, long before it shows up on the timer.
