Good games for 3 players give each person a distinct job instead of pairing two against one. A co-op bomb defusal round does this: one Defuser handles the bomb, and two Experts each own half of the manual. Nobody waits for a turn, nobody is ganged up on, and the same split stretches to four or five players.

## Why games for 3 players are hard to balance

Three-player games are hard to balance because most games are built for pairs or teams. With three, one person often ends up alone against two, or two players compete while the third becomes the kingmaker who decides who wins. A good three-player game gives every seat a different role that the other two depend on.

Odd numbers cause trouble in a few familiar ways. Team games need equal sides, so one person sits out or a team of one faces a team of two. Head-to-head games turn into a pile-on, where the two trailing players gang up on the leader. Turn-based games stretch the wait between turns, and a third player spends most of the evening watching.

Cooperative games avoid all three problems, but only when the roles are genuinely different. If all three people do the same task, the fastest one does it and the others watch. The fix is asymmetry: each player knows something the others do not, and the group has to pass that knowledge around to win.

Bomb Appétit is built on exactly that kind of split. One player sees the bomb and cannot read the manual. The other players read the manual and cannot see the bomb. At three, that gives one person on the bomb and two people sharing the rules, and every one of them is needed in every minute of the round.

## One Defuser and two Experts: how the roles split at three

At three players, one person is the Defuser and two are Experts. The Defuser sees the bomb, describes each module and does what the Experts say. The two Experts hold the manual, ask questions and give instructions. Neither side can finish a module alone, so all three are busy for the whole round.

Here is what each seat does in practice:

- **The Defuser** is the only player who can touch the bomb. They read the edgework first, which is the serial number, batteries, indicators and ports printed on the sides of the case. Then they describe one module at a time and act only when an Expert gives a clear instruction.
- **Expert one** owns about half the module pages. When the Defuser names a module on that list, Expert one asks the questions and gives the answer.
- **Expert two** owns the other half, and also takes notes the whole team needs, such as the edgework and any counts that carry across stages.

Having two Experts is a real step up from one. With a single Expert, the same person has to find the page, read the rule and talk, so every module waits in a queue. With two, one Expert can be finding the next page while the other is still talking the Defuser through the current module. The post on a [2 player bomb defuse game with one Expert](/blog/2-player-bomb-defuse-game-one-expert) covers the solo-Expert setup in full, and the short answer to [whether playing as a pair works](/blog/2-player-bomb-defuse-game-one-expert#does-a-2-player-bomb-defuse-game-work-with-one-expert) is in its first section.

Three players also fit in a small space. One laptop for the bomb and two phones for the manual are enough, and in an online room the group can be in three different cities, joined by a five-letter room code and a call.

## Splitting the manual between two Experts

Splitting the manual means giving each Expert a fixed list of module pages before the round starts. Bomb Appétit has 11 regular modules and 3 needy modules, and each has its own page in the manual. Divide them by effort, not by count, and give each Expert one or two needy pages to watch.

The manual itself recommends this: split the pages between Experts so that two people never search for the same thing. The point is speed. When the Defuser says "a panel with a maze," only one Expert should start turning pages.

The table below is an example split. It balances the modules that need long back-and-forth (Labyrinth, Wire Panels, Recall) against quicker ones, and keeps related pages together. The second column shows how the same pages could go to three Experts when you have four players.

| Module                  | Two Experts (example)   | Three Experts (example) |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Wires                   | Expert A                | Expert A                |
| Big Button              | Expert A                | Expert A                |
| Tangled Wires           | Expert A                | Expert A                |
| Wire Panels             | Expert A                | Expert A                |
| Labyrinth               | Expert A                | Expert C                |
| Glyph Keypad            | Expert B                | Expert B                |
| Color Echo              | Expert B                | Expert B                |
| Word Grid               | Expert B                | Expert B                |
| Recall                  | Expert B                | Expert B                |
| Blinker                 | Expert B                | Expert C                |
| Passcode                | Expert B                | Expert C                |
| Pressure Vent (needy)   | Expert A                | Expert A                |
| Discharge Lever (needy) | Expert B                | Expert B                |
| Dial Alignment (needy)  | Expert A                | Expert C                |
| Edgework appendix       | Expert B writes it down | Expert C writes it down |

Why these groupings work:

- **All the wire pages sit with one person.** Wires, Tangled Wires and Wire Panels all ask about wire colors, so one Expert gets fluent in how the Defuser describes them.
- **Wire Panels needs a running count.** Its page tells the Expert to keep a separate count for each color across four panels. That Expert should have a pen.
- **Recall needs memory.** It runs over five stages, and the team has to remember earlier presses. Give it to the Expert who likes taking notes.
- **Labyrinth and Passcode are slow.** In the three-Expert split they go to the person with the shortest list, so one long module does not block the others.

Write the split on a sticky note or say it aloud before the timer starts. Use the same split for several rounds so each Expert learns their pages. If you play on paper, the pages can be physically divided: print the [standard manual](/manual/1) and hand each Expert their stack.

## What changes with four or five players at one bomb?

With four or five players, the bomb still has one Defuser, and the extra people become Experts. Bomb Appétit supports up to four Experts. More Experts means each one owns fewer pages and can find them faster, but it also means more voices, so the rule about one speaker per module matters more.

The how-to-play page puts it plainly: more Experts means more opinions. At four players, the three-column split above works well. At five, a fourth Expert can take over the edgework and the needy modules entirely, acting as a watcher who answers the Pressure Vent's yes-or-no question and reminds the Defuser to hold the Discharge Lever while the other three solve the regular modules.

Other changes at four and five:

- **Bigger bombs feel better.** A three-module bomb with four Experts leaves most of them idle. Missions with six or more modules give everyone pages to own.
- **Rotation takes longer.** With five people, everyone defusing once means five rounds. Plan an evening around that; the [adults' game night plan](/blog/game-night-ideas-for-adults-bomb-defusal) lays out a full running order for five.
- **Online rooms help.** Online, Experts see the timer, the strike count and how many modules are solved, never the bomb. Each player picks Defuser or Expert when they join, so a bigger group sorts itself quickly.

The setup itself does not change. You still need one screen for the bomb, the manual on other devices or on paper, and a way to talk.

## Which missions are sized for three people?

No mission is labelled for a player count, but the module count and time limit tell you which ones suit two Experts. Missions with three to six modules and five to seven minutes give each Expert enough work without overloading anyone. Mixed plate, Rush hour and Full boil fit three players well.

This is planning advice, not a game rule. Bomb Appétit has 20 missions in four sections, and every one can be played with two to five people. Here is how the module counts line up with a team of one Defuser and two Experts:

- **Warm-ups with one or two modules** (First bite, Button up). One Expert would be enough. With three players, let the second Expert handle the edgework, or let them be the next Defuser.
- **Three-module missions** (Strange symbols, Table for three, Echo chamber, Word salad, Second helpings, Signal fire, Spaghetti). These suit a pair of Experts who are still learning their pages. Despite its name, Table for three refers to three modules, not three players.
- **Five to eight modules** (Mixed plate at 5 modules and 7 minutes, Rush hour at 6 modules and 6 minutes, Full boil at 6 modules plus a needy one and 7 minutes, Back of house at 8 modules plus 2 needy ones and 9 minutes). This is where two Experts shine. The Rush hour description says to split the manual between Experts.
- **The big bombs** (The works with all 11 modules in 11 minutes, No substitutions with only two strikes, Double shift with 14 modules and 3 needy ones on a bigger case, and the final mission with one strike and a new rule seed). Three can do these, but four or five will have an easier time.

The [missions page](/missions) lists every mission with its time limit and modules, so you can pick a short set before the group sits down.

## The three-player mistake: both Experts answering at once

The most common three-player mistake is both Experts answering the same question at the same time. The Defuser hears two instructions, sometimes different ones, and acts on whichever was louder. The fix is simple: the Expert who owns the page speaks, and the other stays quiet unless they spot an error.

This happens because two Experts with one manual each can both look up any page. When the Defuser describes a module, both scroll to it. Both find the rule. Both speak. If they agree, it wastes time. If one has misread the rule, the Defuser has to choose, and a wrong choice is a strike that speeds up the timer.

Three habits stop it:

1. **Own the page.** Use the split from earlier. If the module is not on your list, do not read its page during the round.
2. **Check, then speak once.** The non-owner can read along silently. If they see a mistake, they say "wait" before the Defuser acts, not a second version of the instruction.
3. **Repeat back.** The Defuser says the instruction back before acting. That gives both Experts one more chance to catch an error.

It helps to agree on names for odd symbols before the first glyph appears, so both Experts and the Defuser use the same word.
