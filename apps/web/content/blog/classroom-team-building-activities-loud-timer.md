Classroom team building activities work best when a whole group answers to one clock. A free browser bomb gives each group of three to five students a ticking timer, one player who sees the bomb and others who hold the rules. Six bombs keep thirty students busy, and every role needs every other role to finish.

## Why classroom team building activities work better with a shared timer

A shared timer gives a student group one deadline that nobody can negotiate with. Instead of the teacher calling time, the bomb counts down in front of them, so the pressure belongs to the group and not to an adult. Students organise themselves because waiting costs them seconds they can watch disappear.

Many team building games for students ask a group to build a tower or solve a riddle with no real deadline. The confident students finish the task while the rest watch, and the activity ends when the teacher says so. Nothing in the task pushes the group to share the work.

Bomb Appétit changes that by splitting information. One student, the Defuser, sees the bomb and cannot read the rules. The rest, the Experts, have the manual and cannot see the bomb. Neither side can finish alone, and the timer keeps running while they work it out. A mistake is a strike, and strikes speed the countdown up, so a careless guess is felt by the whole group within seconds.

The noise is part of the appeal. The bomb ticks, a strike makes a sound, and a defusal usually gets a cheer from the table. That is fine in a classroom built for group work and a problem in one that is not, so the next section covers how to spread the noise out.

## Keeping thirty students busy with six bombs

Thirty students fit into six groups of five, each with its own bomb, which is the largest group the game allows: one Defuser and four Experts. Give each group one screen and its own printed manual, seat groups apart, and start them a minute or two apart so the strikes and explosions do not all land together.

The table below is an example layout for a classroom with six group tables. Swap the devices for whatever your school has, but keep one bomb screen per group.

| Group (example) | Where it sits                       | Bomb device                        | Printed manual                          | Start time |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Group 1         | Front left corner                   | One laptop, screen facing the wall | Four copies of manual 1 on the table    | Minute 0   |
| Group 2         | Front right corner                  | One laptop, screen facing the wall | Four copies of manual 1                 | Minute 1   |
| Group 3         | Middle left, against the side wall  | One tablet propped on a stand      | Two copies, pages split between Experts | Minute 2   |
| Group 4         | Middle right, against the side wall | One tablet on a stand              | Two copies, pages split                 | Minute 3   |
| Group 5         | Back left corner                    | One laptop, screen facing the wall | Four copies of manual 1                 | Minute 4   |
| Group 6         | Back right corner                   | One laptop, screen facing the wall | Four copies of manual 1                 | Minute 5   |

### Why the layout works

Corners and walls let each Defuser turn the screen away from the Experts without twisting in a chair. They also put the most distance between groups, so one group's countdown is not the loudest thing another group hears. Keep the middle of the room clear so you can walk between tables.

Staggering starts by a minute means the six timers end at six different moments. A cheer from Group 1 lands while Group 4 is still mid-sentence, which is far easier on everyone than six explosions at once.

### What the other students do

In this layout no one sits out. Every student is either holding the bomb or holding pages. When a group finishes early, rotate the Defuser and replay the same mission, so a quick group does not spend the leftover minutes distracting a slow one.

## What does each role teach a student?

The Defuser role teaches a student to describe what they see in a way someone else can act on. The Expert role teaches a student to ask for the facts a rule needs and to give one clear instruction. Together they teach a group to check understanding before anyone acts.

### What the Defuser learns

The Defuser is the only student who can touch the bomb. They see modules, the small puzzle panels on the case, and the edgework, the labels on the case's sides: serial number, batteries, indicators and ports. They have no idea which of these details matter until an Expert asks.

That teaches patience and order. A Defuser who blurts "there's a button and some wires and a thing" gets three questions back. A Defuser who works through the case one panel at a time, left to right, gets an answer. Students feel the difference in seconds.

### What an Expert learns

Experts hold the manual, which has a page for every module and an appendix on edgework. The game's own advice is to split the pages so that two Experts never search for the same thing. That is a small exercise in dividing work: four students agree who owns which pages before the clock starts, then trust each other to stay in their lane.

Experts also learn that a correct answer said badly is still a strike. "Cut the blue one" fails when there are two blue wires. The Expert has to phrase the instruction so that it can only mean one thing.

### What the group learns together

Because the bomb is shared, a strike is the group's mistake, not one student's. Groups that do well tend to settle a few habits early: the Defuser describes before anyone asks, one Expert speaks at a time, and the Defuser repeats each instruction before acting. Students reach those habits on their own, because the bomb punishes every alternative.

### Talking about it afterwards

A round only teaches as much as the conversation after it. For questions that get a group to talk about what happened without pointing at a person, the guide to [running a debrief after team building communication games](/blog/team-building-communication-games-bomb-debrief) gives a question list in three groups that works in a classroom as well as at work.

## What devices does a classroom bomb round need?

Each group needs one device for the bomb and a manual for the Experts, either printed or on other screens. The game runs in recent versions of Chrome, Firefox, Safari or Edge on a computer, tablet or phone. Nothing is installed and no student needs an account, because guests can play every mission.

### The bomb screen

The bomb is easiest to handle on a bigger screen, so give each Defuser a laptop or a tablet rather than a phone where you can. The Defuser opens [Play](/play), picks the mission and starts it. The game offers a 3D and a 2D view of the bomb, so if a school laptop struggles with one, try the other.

### The manual

Paper is the simplest choice for a room of thirty. It keeps the Experts' eyes off the bomb screen, it needs no extra devices, and students can point at a line. Open the [standard manual for rule seed 1](/manual/1) and print it with the browser's print command. For the print steps and how to check that the seed on the paper matches the bomb, see [printing the manual from the browser](/blog/free-printable-game-manual-bomb-appetit#printing-the-manual-from-the-browser).

If you want to plan the whole round around paper, the post on [print-and-play games with one screen and a stack of pages](/blog/print-and-play-games-one-screen-stack-of-pages) covers splitting printed pages between Experts and keeping a set organised.

### Online rooms

Online rooms, joined with a five-letter code, are built for players in different places. Each player joins on their own device, and Experts see only the timer, the strike count and how many modules are solved. A classroom where every student has a device can use them, but one bomb screen and paper is less to manage.

### Sound

The bomb has its own sounds. In a shared room you may want the device volume low, so each group hears its own bomb without hearing the other five. Test the volume on one device before the class arrives.

## The classroom mistake: groups of six around one bomb

The game caps a bomb at five players: one Defuser and up to four Experts. Squeezing six students around one bomb breaks that, and it also breaks the activity, because the sixth student has nothing to read and nothing to touch. Make a seventh group or use groups of four.

With thirty students, six groups of five divide evenly. With thirty-two, two groups of four and four groups of six might look tidy, but those six-student groups leave someone idle. Make eight groups of four instead, or six groups of five and one group of two. A pair still plays: one Defuser and one Expert, holding all the pages.

### Why smaller groups often work better

A group of three or four gives every student more airtime. With two Experts, each owns half the manual and has to speak often. With four Experts, the reading spreads thin on short missions, and the quietest student can drift. If your devices allow it, groups of four are a good middle size.

### Why bigger is not more fun

Teachers sometimes enlarge groups to save devices. The result is a crowd around a laptop, with two students leaning in to see the screen and two at the back chatting. The Defuser's screen is supposed to be private, and a crowd makes that impossible.

## Classroom bomb round checklist for teachers

This checklist covers the day itself: what to prepare before students arrive, what to say in the first minutes, and what to watch during the rounds. It assumes groups of three to five, one bomb screen per group and printed manuals for rule seed 1.

### The day before

- Print the standard manual, enough copies for each group's Experts.
- Check that the seed printed on the pages is 1.
- Open one mission on a classroom device and confirm the bomb loads and makes sound.
- Decide the groups and write the Defuser rotation for each one.
- Pick the missions. Table for three and Quick snack each run four minutes; [How to play](/how-to-play) explains the timer and strikes if you want to read ahead.

### Before students arrive

- Set out tables in corners and along walls, with each bomb screen facing a wall.
- Put the printed pages on each table, face down.
- Write the start time for each group on the board, a minute apart.

### The first five minutes

- Explain the two roles in two sentences: the Defuser sees the bomb, the Experts see the rules.
- Show that a strike speeds up the timer and that the default is three strikes.
- Tell groups to split the manual pages before they start.

### During the rounds

- Walk the room and listen for one precise sentence and one vague one per group.
- Step in only to remind a group whose turn it is to hold the bomb.
- Rotate the Defuser after every round.

### Afterwards

- Give groups a few minutes to say what helped and what they would change.
- Collect the printed pages for next time; the seed stays the same, so they reuse well.
- The [FAQ](/faq) answers the questions students tend to ask afterwards, such as whether they can play at home without an account.
