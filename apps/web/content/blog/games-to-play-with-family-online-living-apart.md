Good games to play with family online give every household a job and need nothing installed. A browser bomb room does both: one relative holds the bomb, the rest read the manual from their own homes, and a five-letter code plus an ordinary phone call connects up to five people across different cities and time zones.

## Why games to play with family online need no download

Family games played online should need no download because the hardest part of a long-distance evening is getting every household onto the same thing at once. Every extra install, account or update is a chance for one relative to drop out before the game begins. A web page with a code skips all of that.

Picture the usual call. One cousin is on a work laptop that blocks installs. A grandparent has a tablet and a phone that a grandchild set up years ago. A parent is on a phone in the kitchen. Ask all three to download the same app, create the same kind of account and update to the same version, and you lose the first half hour.

Bomb Appétit avoids that. It runs in the browser on any recent version of Chrome, Firefox, Safari or Edge, on a computer, tablet or phone. It is free, and guests can play everything without an account. Signing in with GitHub is optional and only adds leaderboards and saved progress. The [frequently asked questions](/faq) list the devices and the cost in plain terms.

The game is a cooperative bomb defusal. One player, the Defuser, sees a ticking bomb with small puzzle panels called modules. Everyone else is an Expert who holds the manual and never sees the bomb. Nobody can win without talking, which is exactly what a family on a call wants to be doing anyway.

There is a second reason the browser matters. A relative who is nervous about technology can be sent one link, before the evening, and practice opening it. If the link opens, the setup works. There is no separate step to fail.

## Setting up a family bomb room and a phone call

A family bomb room takes five steps: the host opens the play page and creates a room, shares the five-letter code, everyone joins with it, each person picks Defuser or Expert, and the family gets on a call before the host starts the bomb. The game has no voice chat of its own, so the call is separate.

The room steps come straight from the [how to play page](/how-to-play). There is one Defuser per bomb. Online, the Experts see the timer, the strike count and how many modules are solved, but never the bomb itself. That split is what forces the talking.

The call can be whatever the family already uses. A phone call on speaker works. So does any video call. If the call and the game share one phone, though, the screen gets crowded, so where you can, put the call on one device and the game on another.

### Example step list: three households, five people

This is an example for a family spread across three homes: two parents with a child at home, a sibling in another city, and two grandparents. That is five people, the most one bomb takes.

1. **The day before**: the sibling, who will host, sends everyone one message with the link to the game, the words "rule seed 1", and the time.
2. **The grandparents print** the standard manual from their browser. It is laid out to print cleanly in black and white. If they have no printer, they open the manual on a tablet instead.
3. **At the start time**, a parent starts a group phone call from the kitchen phone and puts it on speaker. The sibling joins the call on their phone. The grandparents join on their landline or a phone.
4. **The sibling opens the play page on a laptop** and creates a room. The page shows a five-letter code. The sibling reads it out on the call, letter by letter.
5. **The parents join the room** on a laptop at home. The grandparents join on their tablet, or skip joining and read from paper.
6. **Roles**: the sibling picks Defuser and holds the bomb. The parents and the child are Experts. The grandparents are Experts with the printed pages.
7. **Check the seed**: the grandparents read the rule seed off their printout. It should say 1, the same as the bomb.
8. **The sibling starts the bomb** and reads out the case details first: serial number, batteries, indicators and ports.

Five people in three homes, one laptop holding the bomb, and the call on phones. Swap the Defuser each round so every household gets a turn.

For a household that wants FaceTime on the phone and the bomb on a laptop, the guide on [setting up a FaceTime call and a bomb on two devices](/blog/games-to-play-on-facetime-with-friends#setting-up-a-facetime-call-and-a-bomb-on-two-devices) covers that pairing in detail.

## Grandparents as Experts on a family call

Grandparents make strong Experts on a family call because the job is reading a page carefully and saying what it says, which suits people who like paper and dislike fiddly screens. They never touch the bomb. They need a printed manual, a phone, and a clear voice.

The manual is written for exactly that. Its opening tells the Expert to ask what the Defuser sees, tell them what to do, and never look at their screen. It asks the Experts to split the pages so two people never search for the same thing, and to repeat instructions back before the Defuser acts.

A printed manual is often better for a grandparent than a tablet. Pages can be spread on a table, a pencil can circle a line, and nobody has to scroll while someone shouts a color. The serial number, batteries and indicators can be written in the margin when the Defuser reads them out.

Give the grandparents pages that reward slow, careful reading. The Wires page is a set of short "if" lines read from the top. The Pressure Vent page is a table of short questions with a Yes or No beside each. The edgework appendix, at the back of the manual, describes how to recognise each port, which a grandparent can read out to a young Defuser who is unsure what they are looking at.

Hearing is the thing to plan for. If a grandparent struggles to hear over a speakerphone, give them the pages with fewer words to catch, such as the Glyph Keypad, where the Defuser describes four symbols. Avoid giving them Word Grid, where the whole page turns on words that sound alike and must be spelled.

Grandchildren can help too. A child sitting with the grandparents can read the page with them, which turns a video call into something the two of them do together.

## Which connection problems break a family round, and which do not?

A family round breaks when people cannot hear each other, because every move depends on a spoken description and a spoken instruction. A slow room update matters much less: Experts never see the bomb, and a printed manual needs no connection at all. Protect the call first, then the bomb.

Start with the call, because it carries the whole game. If audio cuts out mid-sentence, a Defuser can act on half an instruction. Agree a rule before the first round: anyone who hears a gap says "say again", and the Defuser waits.

The bomb itself is more forgiving. The project's README explains that the Defuser's browser runs the game engine on that device and records every action, then sends the log when the bomb ends. In practice, the timer and the modules respond on the Defuser's own screen.

What Experts receive from the room is small: the rule seed and a status line with the time, the strike count, the modules solved and whether a needy module is awake. A needy module is one that cannot be solved and wakes up now and then, wanting attention. If that status line lags for a moment, the Experts still have everything they need, because the Defuser says it aloud.

The manual is the part least likely to fail. Print it, and it works with no connection whatsoever. If you use a screen instead, open the manual before the call starts so it is already loaded.

So the order of priority for a slow home connection is simple. Give the household with the best connection the bomb. Give the household with the worst connection paper. Keep the call on the most reliable device in each home.

## Can a child be the Defuser on a family call?

A child can be the Defuser on a family call if they can read letters and numbers aloud and handle the laptop or tablet the bomb runs on. The job is describing, not knowing rules. Start them on the first mission, which has one module and five minutes, and keep an adult nearby who does not read the manual.

The Defuser seat is about talking. The child describes each module, reads the six-character serial number, counts batteries and waits for an instruction before cutting or pressing. The manual explains that the serial number never uses the letters I or O, so a round character is a zero and a straight one is a one. That is useful to tell a child before the round.

The [missions page](/missions) lists the early missions in order. First bite is one Wires module with five minutes on the clock. Button up adds a big button. Both allow three strikes, and each strike speeds the countdown up, which children tend to find funny rather than frightening when they expect it.

One module needs no Expert at all: on the Discharge Lever, the Defuser holds the lever down to drain a meter and lets go when it is empty. A young child can manage it alone, but it only appears on later missions that include needy modules.

For which manual pages a child can take on the other side of the call, the guide to [which modules a younger Expert can read](/blog/family-game-night-ideas-one-screen-and-a-manual#which-modules-a-younger-expert-can-read) works through each page by what it asks.

An adult in the room with the child should stay out of the manual and off the bomb's buttons. Their job is to help the child hear the call and keep the device steady.

## The family online mistake: everyone on one laptop

The most common online family mistake is running the call, the bomb and the manual on a single laptop in one home. The Defuser's screen then shows the manual, or the Experts in that room see the bomb, and the game stops working. Each role needs its own screen or paper.

The game is built on a split. The Defuser sees the bomb and cannot see the manual. The Experts have the manual and cannot see the bomb. If one laptop shows both, the household gathered round it can solve every module without saying a word, and the relatives in other cities become spectators.

It usually happens because one home has only one good computer. The answer is the printer, or a phone. The manual reads well on anything, so a phone held by an Expert in the same room as the Defuser works, as long as that Expert turns away from the bomb's screen.

A shared laptop for the call alone is fine. The problem is only when the bomb and the manual meet on the same screen or within view of each other.

Getting this right is what makes the evening feel like playing together rather than watching one house play. Every household holds a piece nobody else has, and that is why a shared loss stings less.
