The bomb game manual in Bomb Appétit runs in a fixed order: a short page on how to use it, one page per module in the same order every time, an appendix on edgework, and a glossary. Experts find the right line fastest by asking the Defuser what they see, then jumping straight to that module's page.

## What the bomb game manual holds, page by page

The manual has seventeen sections. One opening page tells the Experts how to work, fourteen module pages follow in catalog order, then an edgework appendix and a glossary close it. The order never changes, so an Expert who learns where Labyrinth sits can find it again in any copy, printed or on screen.

Bomb Appétit is a bomb game with a manual at its center: the Defuser holds the bomb, and everyone else holds these pages and nothing more. That makes the manual the Experts' whole view of the game, and knowing its shape matters as much as knowing any one rule.

Here is the table of contents in order, with what an Expert should be hunting for on each page.

## How is a module page in the manual laid out?

A module page always starts the same way. A small label above the heading says "Module" or "Needy module". The heading is the module's name. A lead paragraph then describes the panel the Defuser is looking at and the method in plain steps. The rules come after that, as a list, a table, a drawing or a mix.

The lead paragraph is the part new Experts skip, and it is the part that saves them. It says how things are counted: wires from the top starting at one, Recall positions from the left, Labyrinth columns from the left and rows from the top. It says what counts as a tap on the Big Button. It says what a wrong action does, for example that a Recall strike sends the module back to stage 1 with new numbers. Reading those two or three sentences once, before the first round, means the Expert asks the right question instead of an almost right one.

Below the lead, a page uses one of four rule shapes.

- **Ordered lists.** Wires and Big Button give numbered lines. The page tells you to read from the top and stop at the first line that is true. Every list ends with an "Otherwise" line that catches the rest.
- **Lookup tables.** Color Echo, Word Grid, Recall, Wire Panels, Pressure Vent and Dial Alignment are tables. You find a row from one fact and read the answer across.
- **Drawings.** Glyph Keypad prints each glyph next to its name. Labyrinth prints nine mazes. Tangled Wires prints a diagram of overlapping loops with the same letters repeated in a table underneath, so an Expert who dislikes the diagram can use the table.
- **Word lists.** Passcode is a list to cross words off. Blinker pairs a code table with a word list.

The pages are written for someone reading aloud under pressure, so sentences are short and each one gives one instruction. Colors are named in words as well as shown, which keeps a black and white printout usable.

For what each puzzle shows on the bomb and how hard it is to learn, the companion guide to [the 11 regular modules](/blog/bomb-defusal-puzzle-game-11-modules-explained) goes through them one at a time. The three pages labelled "Needy module" work differently from the rest, and the post on [needy modules that never stay solved](/blog/needy-modules-never-stay-solved) explains why they keep coming back.

## The edgework appendix and the glossary

The appendix explains the labels printed on the bomb's casing, which the manual calls edgework: the serial number, the batteries, the indicators and the ports. The glossary defines seven words the rest of the manual leans on. Both sit at the back, after the last module page, and neither changes from one manual to the next.

The appendix opens with its own advice: ask for all of the edgework once, at the start, and write it down. Then it gives one short entry per label.

- **Serial number.** Six characters, always ending in a digit. The letters I and O are never used, so anything round is a zero and anything straight is a one. The only vowels that can appear are A, E and U.
- **Batteries.** Between zero and four holders. A holder carries one battery or a pack of two. A rule counts batteries unless it says holders.
- **Indicators.** A three-letter label next to a light that is lit or unlit. The appendix lists all eleven possible labels, from YUM to MSG.
- **Ports.** A table of the six port types, each with its drawing, its name and one sentence on how to recognise it. Coax, for instance, is one large ring with a solid dot in the middle.

The port table is the entry Experts use most during play. A Defuser who sees "a box with a thick slot" can be matched to a name in seconds, without anyone needing to know what the port is for.

The glossary is short on purpose. It defines Defuser, Expert, module, needy module, strike, edgework and rule seed, each in one sentence. Two of those definitions settle arguments that come up in a first game. A strike speeds up the countdown, and the last allowed strike sets the bomb off. A bomb only works with the manual that has its rule seed. Pointing a new player at those lines is quicker than explaining them from memory.

## Finding the right line while the clock runs

Speed in the manual comes from asking before reading. The Defuser names the module and describes it, the Expert goes straight to that page, finds the one fact that picks a row or a list, and reads only that part aloud. Scanning a whole page while the Defuser waits is the slow way.

On a screen, the fastest route to a page is the keyboard. Press the slash key to put the cursor in the "Find a module" box, type the first few letters of the module's name, and press Enter. The j key jumps to the next section and k to the previous one, which helps when two modules are being solved one after the other.

Here is an example exchange for a Big Button. The values are only an example.

1. Defuser: "Big Button. It's blue and the word on it is HUSH."
2. Expert presses slash, types "big", presses Enter, and lands on the Big Button page.
3. Expert reads the numbered list from the top, checking each line against the color, the word and the edgework already written down.
4. Expert stops at the first line that is true and says "tap" or "hold".
5. If the answer is hold, the Expert waits for the Defuser to name the strip color, then reads the digit from the table under "Letting go of a held button".

Notice that the Expert never reads lines that come after the first true one. Those lines are for other buttons and other bombs. Reading them out loud only gives the Defuser something to mishear.

Groups with more than one Expert move faster when they split the pages. The manual's opening page says to divide them so that two people never search for the same thing. A simple split gives one person the first seven module pages and the other the rest, with whoever holds the edgework note answering every edgework question. Whoever is holding the needy pages listens for the Defuser calling one out.

Keep the edgework written down where every Expert can see it. Four module pages can ask about it, and asking the Defuser again in the middle of a module costs more time than the first reading did.

## Does the manual change between rule seeds?

The rules change with the seed, but the layout does not. Every manual has the same seventeen sections in the same order, with the same headings, the same lead paragraphs, the same appendix and the same glossary. What changes is the content of the lists and tables: the conditions, the answers, the mazes, the word lists and the questions.

A rule seed is a whole number from 1 to 999,999, and each one builds a complete manual. Seed 1 is the standard manual, and its opening page says "(standard)" next to the number. The standard manual is built ahead of time. Any other seed is generated the first time someone opens it, then shows the same pages to everyone who asks for that seed.

Because the structure holds still, a team's habits carry from one seed to the next. An Expert who knows that Color Echo has two tables split by vowel will still look for two tables on a new seed. Only the colors in the cells will be different. The Discharge Lever page is the one page that reads the same everywhere, because it has no rules to generate.

How the seed turns into rules, and why the generator rejects some manuals before anyone sees them, is the subject of a separate post on [games with random rules](/blog/games-with-random-rules-rule-seed-manual). Its section on [how a rule seed generates every manual page](/blog/games-with-random-rules-rule-seed-manual#how-a-rule-seed-generates-every-manual-page) shows what the seed decides and what it leaves alone.

## The manual mistake: reading the page for the wrong seed

The most expensive manual error is reading the right page from the wrong manual. The layout matches, the headings match, the Expert finds the line with confidence, and the answer is wrong. The opening page warns that a different number means different rules, and that these pages will get you blown up.

The mistake is easy to make because two manuals look the same at a glance. A printed copy of seed 1 lying on the table will happily answer questions about a bomb built on another seed. Nothing on the paper will look out of place.

Every place the game starts a bomb also tells you which manual it needs.

- The [missions page](/missions) lists "manual" and a number beside every mission.
- The play setup screen has an "Open manual" button that names the seed.
- An online room's lobby says which manual the Experts will use, and once the bomb is armed, the Expert's screen loads that manual by itself.
- In freeplay, the setup form has a field labelled "Manual (rule seed)".

Most missions use manual 1. The last one does not. The mission called Bomb Appétit uses seed 2026, a manual nobody has practised on, so a team that has printed seed 1 and stopped checking will walk straight into it.

Make the seed check a habit before every bomb. The Defuser reads the number from the setup screen, and an Expert reads the number at the top of the manual, either on screen or on the first printed sheet. If they match, start. If anyone switches to a new seed between rounds, the check happens again. The [standard manual](/manual/1) is the place to start for anyone who has not played before.
